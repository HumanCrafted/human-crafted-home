The clean-top-edge bit. Down cut geometry shears downward into the workpiece, pressing the surface fibres into the material instead of lifting them — which is what makes it the right choice for veneered plywood, melamine, and figured hardwood where the top face is the face that shows.

Stepdowns are roughly half what the same-diameter upcut takes, and that is deliberate — see below.

Bits&Bits publish 16–18k RPM at 150–180 ipm for wood and MDF, which works out to about 0.005" per tooth. That is a sensible number for a machine that cannot feed quickly; the X-Carve Pro can, and its spindle drops to 8,000 rpm, so the figures above run a proper chipload instead.

## Notes

**Always ramp — never plunge.** A down cut bit augers chips *downward*, so a straight plunge packs the hole beneath the cutter with its own waste and the bit rides on compacted dust. Plunge feeds above are set low as a safety net, but the correct entry is a ramp or a helix.

**Chip evacuation is the limiting factor, not cutting force.** In a deep slot the chips have nowhere to go, so they pack, heat builds, and the edge burns — which in maple means scorch marks you cannot sand out at depth. Halving the stepdown gives each pass a shallower channel to clear.

**Not the bit for deep through-cuts.** Use [[bit-81618|U01]] for those and accept the tearout on the top face, or rough with the upcut and take a finishing pass with this one.

Avoid it in acrylic if there is any alternative — melted chips get pressed back into the cut and weld to the wall.
